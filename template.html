Project Timucua

Did Timucua leave its words in the Seminole languages?

Timucua was spoken across north Florida and south Georgia until the early 1700s, then fell silent. The Seminole and Miccosukee peoples who came to Florida afterwards speak Mikasuki and Muscogee, two Muskogean languages. This database lines up their basic vocabulary with Timucua's, word by word, to ask whether any Timucua survived the handover.

The short answer, from a pre-registered test: no layer large enough to see. The long answer, and every word behind it, is below.

What the comparison found

The test asked a narrow question. Take the Mikasuki words that have no cognate anywhere else in the Muskogean family, the residue, and ask whether they resemble Timucua more than they resemble the words of fifty unrelated languages from around the world. If Timucua speakers had left vocabulary behind, it should hide in that residue.

    Residue. A word with no cognate in any other Muskogean language. Mikasuki has 20 such concepts out of 95; 15 of them are attested in Timucua and were tested.

    Pre-registered. The protocol, data and code were frozen and fingerprinted (SHA-256) at 15:01 UTC on 10 October 2026, before any Timucua similarity was computed.

    A null only means something if the method can find contact where contact is known to exist. It can, but only when the contact runs deep. The same design picks out inherited vocabulary and pidgins at enormous strength, yet it misses the documented borrowing between Creek and two languages that lived inside the Creek Confederacy, because their wordlists share only 33 to 38 concepts.

    z score. How far a language pair's similarity sits above what shuffled word pairings give, in standard deviations. Above about 2 is the usual hint of a signal, but unrelated pairs reach it too, which is why the decision rule also ranks each pair against 50 stand-in languages.

    Every test and its verdict

    All 32 aggregate tests in the pilot, including the calibration runs. Rule set A passes a test only if the permutation p is below 0.05 and the pair ranks first or second of 51 in both directions (p_max < 0.05).

    Candidate loanwords

    No Mikasuki or Creek word reached even an uncorrected p of 0.05. The only lead runs the other way: seven Timucua words sit unusually close to widespread Muskogean forms, against one of fifty placebo languages doing as well. The strongest are efa 'dog' beside ifa, neha 'grease' beside niha, and ilake 'night' beside niɬaki. This was not pre-registered, so it is a question for specialists rather than a finding.

    Gatschet, 1884. "The Maskoki word ifa, dog, went over from them to the Timucua" (A Migration Legend of the Creek Indians, p. 58).

    The languages

    Sixty-seven wordlists: Timucua, the Muskogean family, a handful of calibration languages, and a control panel of fifty languages chosen to sound like Mikasuki while having no possible contact with it, one per family and none from North America.

    Show the fifty control languages

    How it was done

      SCA similarity. One minus the distance between two words after aligning them by sound class (List 2012), so ifa and efa score high even though the vowels differ.

      Two-way design. Every candidate pair is compared with fifty stand-in donors and fifty stand-in recipients at once, so a language that resembles everything cannot pass by resembling Mikasuki.

      Checking the method before trusting it

      The pipeline was first run where the answer is known. Against seven languages with documented Spanish loans in the World Loanword Database, it found the loan layer every time (p = 0.0001) and picked out individual loans with a precision of 0.92 to 1.00. It also showed a problem: the plain permutation test in the original proposal flags 8 to 30 per cent of unrelated language pairs. The two-way rule brought that to about 2 per cent before any Timucua comparison existed.

      How often each rule flags pairs with no contact
      RuleFalse positives
      Permutation test alone8 to 10% on residue sets; 18 to 30% on full lists
      Original proposal: permutation, beat the primary control, beat the panel's 95th percentile2 to 4% on residue sets; up to 12% on full lists
      Final rule: permutation, plus first or second of 51 as donor and as recipient1.5 to 2.3% overall; at most 4% in any row or column

      Power is the other limit. With real Spanish loans planted into a 100-concept list, the test finds two loans half the time, three loans 77 per cent of the time and four 88 per cent of the time. At the planned 350 concepts it finds six loans 83 per cent of the time. Spanish loans are recent and transparent, so these are best cases; old Timucua loans would be harder to see.

      What would change the answer. A fuller Timucua lexicon. The ASJP list gives 87 Timucua concepts, and only George Aaron Broadwell's Timucua–English dictionary can take the comparison to 350.

      Using the data

      Everything on this page comes from the repository's cldf/ folder, a Cross-Linguistic Data Formats wordlist that validates with cldf validate, and from its SQLite copy in db/.

      Querying it

      import sqlite3
db = sqlite3.connect("db/project-phaistos.sqlite")
db.execute("""select language, original, form, source
              from southeast_forms where concept = 'dog'""").fetchall()

      Or load it with pycldf: Wordlist.from_metadata("cldf/Wordlist-metadata.json"). To rebuild the database and this page from the pilot, run ./build/build.sh.

      Spellings. Every form keeps its source spelling (an ASJP code, or Gatschet's, Haas's or Kimball's own) beside a normalised IPA transcription, and every normalisation rule is logged.

      Sources, licences and care

      The wordlists come from the ASJP database (version 20), with Hitchiti added from Albert Gatschet's A Migration Legend of the Creek Indians (1884) and Mary R. Haas's 1937 field notebook in the American Philosophical Society, and Apalachee from Geoffrey Kimball's "An Apalachee Vocabulary" (1988). Validation used the World Loanword Database and the Intercontinental Dictionary Series; concepts are linked to Concepticon. These datasets are published under CC-BY 4.0; Gatschet is in the public domain. Code is MIT-licensed.

      All material here is published or archival. Following the project's ethics plan, the Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida are being asked for guidance on how this work is described and shared. Language contact is a normal part of every language's history, and nothing here makes a claim about any community's connection to Florida.
